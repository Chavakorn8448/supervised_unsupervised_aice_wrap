When is machine learning the right tool?

✅
Reach for ML when… the rules are too complex or keep changing to write by hand, and you have lots of examples to learn from.
⚠️
Skip ML when… a simple fixed rule already does the job, or you barely have any data.
🌍
Real examples… spam filters, recommendations, voice and image recognition.

2 · Classification Algorithms

A classifier sorts things into categories. Start with KNN's neighbor vote, then compare lines, yes/no questions, forests, and probability evidence on the same red-vs-blue task.

Birds of a feather

If your five closest friends all love football, you can guess you might too. KNN classifies the same way: a new point takes a vote among its k nearest neighbors. First, what does "nearest" even mean? Drag the white point around — lines show the distance to every other point, and the closest few light up.

Controls

Drag the white point. "Distance" is just the straight line between two dots — the same ruler you'd use on a map.

This straight-line distance is called Euclidean distance. Closer = more similar.

When would you actually use KNN?

✅
Reach for it when… your data is small-to-medium, you want something simple and explainable, and the boundary between groups is irregular.
⚠️
Watch out when… data is huge (it compares against every point) or has many features (distance stops meaning much).
🌍
Real examples… "you might also like…" recommenders, handwriting recognition, simple medical lookups.

What the model "believes" everywhere — and the magic of k

Instead of one test point, we can ask KNN about every pixel and paint the result. That coloured map is the decision boundary. Watch what k does to it.

Logistic regression: a soft line between classes

Logistic regression draws a line, then turns distance from that line into a probability. Far on one side is confidently red, far on the other is confidently blue, and near the line is uncertain.

Controls

A steeper slope makes probabilities jump from red to blue faster. The boundary is where the model is 50/50.

When would you actually use logistic regression?

✅
Reach for it when… you need a fast baseline, probabilities, and a fairly straight separation between classes.
⚠️
Watch out when… the real boundary is curved or has many islands; a plain line will underfit.
🌍
Real examples… churn risk, loan default, disease screening, and click prediction.

The threshold: when does probability become a class?

Logistic regression outputs a probability. The threshold is the rule that converts it into red or blue: above the threshold means blue, below means red.

Controls

Watch out when… the tree gets deep; it can memorize the training data.

Real examples… triage rules, loan decisions, support routing, and fraud flags.

Naive Bayes: evidence from each feature

Naive Bayes asks: "How likely is this x-value for red? How likely is this y-value for red?" It combines those pieces of evidence and compares red against blue.

Controls

"Naive" means it treats features as independent clues. That is often wrong, but surprisingly useful.

When would you actually use Naive Bayes?

✅
Reach for it when… you need a very fast classifier and features act like separate clues.
⚠️
Watch out when… features strongly depend on each other; the "naive" assumption can mislead it.
🌍
Real examples… spam filtering, sentiment, document tagging, and quick medical triage.

When would you actually use regression?

✅
Reach for it when… you're predicting a number and the trend is roughly a straight line — and you want a formula you can read.
⚠️
Watch out when… the relationship is curved, or a few outliers can drag the whole line.
🌍
Real examples… house prices, sales forecasts, predicting exam scores from study hours.

Why this line and not another?

Out of infinite possible lines, what makes one "best"? We measure how wrong a line is by adding up its errors — the cost. Tilt and shift the line yourself and try to make the cost as small as possible.

How the machine finds it: gradient descent

The computer doesn't guess randomly. It rolls "downhill" on the cost — a tiny step at a time toward less error. That's gradient descent, the engine behind nearly all of modern AI, including neural networks. Press play and watch the line learn.

Controls

Too small a learning rate = painfully slow. Too big = it overshoots and bounces. Sound familiar? It's the same trade-off as k.

KNN regressor: average the nearby answers

KNN can predict numbers too. Instead of taking a class vote, it finds the k nearest x-values and averages their y-values.

Controls

Near points matter; far points are ignored. The prediction is a local average, not a line.

When would you actually use KNN regression?

✅
Reach for it when… similar examples should have similar numeric answers and the data is small enough to search.
⚠️
Watch out when… data is huge, noisy, or has too many features for distance to stay meaningful.
🌍
Real examples… estimating a house price from nearby homes, local demand forecasts, and simple recommendation scores.

4 · Clustering with K-Means

Now we remove the labels. This is unsupervised learning: nobody tells the machine the right answer — it must discover the groups in the data entirely on its own.

K-Means, one step at a time

K-Means repeats two simple moves until nothing changes: assign each point to its nearest center, then update each center to the middle of its points. Step through it slowly.

Controls

🎯 Try this: press New centers a few times. Same data, different starting spots — does K-Means always land on the same groups?

When would you actually use K-Means?

✅
Reach for it when… you have no labels and want to discover natural groups, and you can roughly guess how many there are.
⚠️
Watch out when… clusters are odd shapes or very different sizes, or you truly can't estimate k.
🌍
Real examples… customer segmentation, grouping news articles, compressing the colours in an image.

How many clusters should we ask for?

K-Means needs you to pick k up front — but how? We try several values and measure how "tight" the clusters are. As k grows, tightness improves... then stops improving much. That bend is the elbow, and it's usually the right k.

Pick a k from the elbow

🎯 Try this: find the sharpest bend in the curve on the left. Set k to that value — see how clean the clusters on the right become.

Where this is used: customer segments

A shop knows each customer's income and spending — but not who's who. K-Means reveals natural segments the business can act on (budget shoppers, big spenders, cautious earners…). Run it on real-looking data.

Controls

Each axis is a real customer trait. Press Find segments to let K-Means group similar customers.

This exact technique powers product recommendations, targeted marketing, and even grouping patients by symptoms in medicine.

5 · Ensembles & Model Tuning

You met trees and forests in Module 2. Now we go deeper: how trees overfit, why a forest vote is steadier, and how grid search chooses model settings without cheating.

Depth: useful detail or memorized noise?

Module 2 introduced the decision tree classifier: learned if/else questions that end in a class. Here we focus on the main risk: as depth grows, a tree can shift from capturing the real rule to memorizing tiny quirks.

Controls

Each box is a learned question. More depth means more questions, more leaves, and more ways to fit detail — including detail that may just be noise.

What changes after Module 2?

✅
Module 2 taught… what a tree and forest are, and how they classify points.
⚠️
Module 5 adds… how to diagnose overfitting, compare one model against an ensemble, and tune hyperparameters.
🌍
Real workflow… train several settings, score them on validation data, then keep the model that generalizes best.

Forest voting smooths one-tree mistakes

A single deep tree can produce jagged boxes because every split is based on one version of the data. A forest trains many slightly different trees and averages their votes, so individual mistakes get softened.

Controls

🎯 Try this: set 1 tree at depth 8 to exaggerate overfitting. Now drag trees up to 40 and watch the vote map smooth out.

Grid search: choosing settings without guessing

Depth and tree count are hyperparameters: choices made before training. Grid search tries a small table of combinations, scores each one on validation data, and keeps the winner. Hover a cell to inspect that model; 🏆 is the best.

It is brute force, but reliable. The key idea is not magic search; it is comparing settings fairly on data the model did not train on.

↪ Remember the validation set? We score each model on a held-out validation slice — not the final test set. If we picked the winner using the test set, we'd be peeking at the answers and cheating all over again. The test set is only opened once, at the very end.

6 · Artificial Neural Networks

This is the technology behind image recognition, ChatGPT, and self-driving cars. And it's built from a unit so simple you can understand it completely in five minutes.

Meet one neuron

A neuron multiplies each input by a weight, adds them up with a bias, and fires a signal. That's the whole thing. Slide the weights and watch it react.

Inputs & weights

When would you actually use neural networks?

✅
Reach for it when… you have lots of data and a complex pattern — images, audio, language — and accuracy matters more than explainability.
⚠️
Watch out when… data is small or you must explain every decision; they're powerful but data-hungry "black boxes".
🌍
Real examples… face and voice recognition, language models like ChatGPT, self-driving perception.

The activation function: the neuron's "personality"

After adding up its inputs, a neuron passes the result through an activation function — a curve that decides how it fires. This little non-linear step is the secret that lets stacked neurons learn curved, complex patterns. Hover the curve to read it.

Pick an activation

Sigmoid squashes to 0–1 (a probability). ReLU is "off, or pass-through" — simple and the workhorse of deep learning today.
